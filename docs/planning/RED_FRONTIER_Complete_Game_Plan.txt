RED FRONTIER — COMPLETE GAME PLAN
Coding Foxes | Unity 6 | Browser game | Pixel art
Prepared: 14 November 2026

1. PROJECT SUMMARY

RED FRONTIER is a single-player Mars outpost management and survival game. The player commands humanity’s first permanent research outpost and must keep the crew safe while maintaining life support, energy, food, water, infrastructure, and morale. Each turn advances one Martian sol. Environmental events and mission decisions create trade-offs whose effects can unfold over time.

The intended experience is a polished, scientifically grounded browser game that a judge or first-time player can understand and play within 10–15 minutes. No installation, account, or internet connection during gameplay should be required after the game loads.

Core promise: Humanity’s survival is an engineering problem.

2. LOCKED DESIGN DECISIONS

- Game title: RED FRONTIER.
- Genre: single-player outpost management / survival simulation.
- Engine and language: Unity 6 and C#.
- Target: Unity Web build (WebGL/Web platform), playable in a modern browser.
- Art direction: predominantly pixel art.
- World view: compact 2D isometric outpost, with a stationary orthographic-style presentation. Keep the whole base visible; no camera rotation, panning, or zoom in the first version.
- Input: mouse and touch-friendly interface.
- Simulation: turn-based, one sol at a time; the player chooses when to advance.
- Persistence: local save; no account or online services needed.
- Scope: one compact, complete mission before adding more maps or extensive content.

The conversation explored both a 3D environment and a 2D isometric approach. The complete game-plan decision is to use 2D isometric rendering because it better supports consistent pixel art, faster asset production, simpler interaction, and browser performance. Treat 3D modeling as out of scope for the first playable release.

3. PLAYER EXPERIENCE AND GAME LOOP

Main loop:
1. Review Mission Control: current sol, crew condition, resources, alerts, and forecast.
2. Inspect facilities and understand their production, consumption, condition, and upgrades.
3. Choose priorities or respond to an active mission event.
4. Advance one sol.
5. Resolve resource production and consumption, maintenance, crew needs, and delayed effects.
6. Present a readable summary of what changed and why.
7. Continue until the mission is completed or the outpost can no longer operate; show a debrief.

An active critical decision pauses advancing the next sol until it is resolved. Ordinary informational events need not interrupt play. Explain outcomes clearly so failure feels connected to the player’s choices rather than arbitrary.

The first mission should teach the core systems in a short sequence: establish the outpost, manage a first resource trade-off, respond to an environmental event, and reach a clear mission outcome.

4. GAME SYSTEMS

A. Life support (critical)
- Oxygen / atmosphere and crew health.
- Crew needs and daily consumption.
- Low reserves cause escalating warnings and consequences, not an instant unexplained loss.

B. Energy (critical)
- Solar generation, battery storage, and power distribution.
- Facilities consume power; storms or maintenance issues can reduce generation.
- Make generation, consumption, and reserve runway easy to understand.

C. Food and greenhouse (critical)
- Crop production, food reserves, greenhouse condition, water use, and electricity use.
- Reduced operations conserve power but lower future yields.

D. Water (critical)
- Extraction, purification, recycling, storage, and facility demand.
- Recycling and greenhouse decisions connect water to energy and food.

E. Radiation protection (critical)
- Background exposure and solar particle events.
- Sheltering protects crew; events should distinguish radiation risk to people/electronics from dust effects on solar production.

F. Infrastructure and maintenance (critical)
- Facility condition gradually changes; faults affect output or demand.
- Repairs and upgrades use time, rover capacity, or maintenance materials.

G. Crew wellbeing
- Morale, work availability, and health respond to workload, safety, and recreation.
- Non-critical choices can have meaningful effects without threatening immediate survival.

These systems must interact. For example, reducing greenhouse activity saves electricity and water but reduces future food. Turning down recycling can save energy initially but risks the water reserve.

5. MAP, BUILDINGS, AND ART

Keep the first map to approximately one screen and make every visible facility readable and clickable. Suggested facilities:
- Main Habitat: crew quarters, atmosphere, health.
- Greenhouse: food production; consumes water and power.
- Solar Array / Power Station: generates electricity; affected by dust and maintenance.
- Battery Storage: stores reserve energy.
- Water Processing Facility: extraction, purification, recycling, storage.
- Radiation Shelter: protects crew during radiation events.
- Engineering Workshop: repairs, maintenance materials, upgrades.
- Landing Pad: mission arrival and visual landmark.

Optional visual details after the core is complete: connecting corridors, communications antenna, rover(s), rocks, distant formations, and environmental overlays.

Pixel-art principles:
- Pick a consistent pixel scale, palette, outline style, and light direction before producing assets.
- Create a small reusable tileset and a coherent sprite for each required facility.
- Use layered sprites, shadows, selection highlights, status icons, and restrained animation to create depth.
- Prioritize clear silhouettes and readable facility labels over decoration.
- Keep asset sizes, compression, and animation counts appropriate for a browser build.

6. MISSION DECISIONS AND EVENTS

This is a defining feature: the player balances competing priorities with incomplete information. Choices should rarely have a single obviously correct answer. Avoid punishing a player simply for choosing; make trade-offs legible and outcomes fair.

Event flow:
1. Detect or schedule an event.
2. Show a notification and severity/category.
3. Explain the situation, forecast, and relevant science in plain language.
4. Offer two or three choices with likely costs and benefits.
5. Player selects an action.
6. Simulation validates and applies effects.
7. Record the choice and outcome in mission history.

Event types for the first release (target 10–12 well-designed events):
- Radiation / space weather: 2.
- Dust storms / environment: 2.
- Infrastructure failures: 2.
- Food and water: 2.
- Crew wellbeing: 2.
- Scientific discoveries: 2.

Example trade-offs:
- Solar particle event: shelter crew and postpone work, or send an autonomous rover to attempt maintenance at the cost of rover battery and materials.
- Dust storm: clean panels before arrival using rover resources, or preserve reserves and accept lower energy production.
- Greenhouse temperature failure: spend extra electricity to preserve crops, or reduce greenhouse operations and lose some production while repairs proceed.
- Crew recreation request: improve morale while delaying non-critical work, or keep the schedule and risk reduced morale.

Consequences should support:
- Immediate effects: resource or condition changes on selection.
- Delayed effects: consequences several sols later.
- Conditional effects: depend on prior choices, facility state, or available supplies.
- Positive effects: discoveries, successful repairs, and upgrades can create opportunities.

Store event content as data rather than hardcoding each event into UI code. A reusable event definition should include ID, title, description, category, severity, choices, immediate effects, delayed effects, requirements/conditions, and an educational explanation. The event manager presents events; the simulation engine validates and applies effects. Choices must not write directly to displayed UI values.

Mission history should show prior choices and connect them to later outcomes where possible (for example, postponed maintenance contributing to an energy shortage during a later storm).

7. USER INTERFACE

A. Mission Control (primary screen)
- Isometric map, sol counter, crew status, resource dashboard, forecast, alerts, and Advance Sol action.
- Always show units and explain important changes.

B. Facility panel
- Facility purpose, condition, current output/consumption, maintenance needs, and available actions/upgrades.

C. Decision/event panel
- Situation, severity, choices, expected trade-offs, and science note.
- Keep the game paused for unresolved critical decisions.

D. Mission history
- Timeline of events, player decisions, and consequences.

E. Debrief
- Mission outcome, key resource trends, major decisions, and short educational takeaways.
- Offer restart and return-to-menu actions.

F. Main menu / pause
- Clear Play action, controls/help, audio options if time permits, and local save/resume.

Design for desktop first but ensure panels and controls remain usable on narrow screens and touch devices. The map may scale to fit; UI must not cover essential interactions.

8. GAME RULES AND BALANCING

- Use a single authoritative simulation state and a clear order of operations for each sol.
- Track starting values, production, consumption, reserves, facility condition, crew state, active effects, and mission status.
- Show forecasts and warnings before severe consequences when feasible.
- Use tunable data for resource rates and event effects so balancing does not require rewriting core logic.
- Define explicit completion, success, and failure conditions. Prefer gradual, understandable escalation over sudden hidden failure.
- Provide a short tutorial or contextual hints for the first run.
- Keep scientific facts separate from simplified game rules. Label assumptions and explain abstractions in the Science Center or event notes.

Core modules and ownership:
- GameManager: overall state, scene flow, mission status.
- SimulationManager: advances sols and calls systems in a documented order.
- ResourceManager: validated resource balances and capacity limits.
- LifeSupportSystem: crew needs, atmosphere, and health.
- EnergySystem: generation, demand, storage, and brownouts.
- FoodSystem: greenhouse operation and food reserves.
- WaterSystem: production, recycling, consumption, and reserves.
- InfrastructureSystem: facility condition, faults, maintenance, upgrades.
- EventManager: event selection, availability, and active decisions.
- Effect/Consequence queue: immediate and scheduled effects.
- UI controllers: display state and submit player commands; never own game rules.
- SaveSystem: versioned local save and safe resume.

Use data assets (for example ScriptableObjects or JSON where appropriate) for resources, facilities, event definitions, and balance values. Keep simulation logic testable independently from presentation. Use stable IDs for buildings and events. Keep dependencies simple and document public interfaces before parallel implementation.

10. WORK DISTRIBUTION

For a team of up to six, assign one owner per workstream:
1. Technical Lead / Integrator: Unity configuration, architecture, main scene, repository, integration, builds, code review.
2. Game Systems Developer: resources, life support, energy, food, water, sol progression, win/lose rules.
3. Environment and Pixel Artist: tileset, Mars terrain, facility sprites, visual consistency, small animations.
4. UI/UX Developer: menu, HUD, facility panel, event panel, history, debrief, responsive layout.
5. Science and Research Lead: verified science references, assumptions, scenarios, educational explanations.
6. Gameplay, QA, and Presentation: event writing, balance, bug reports, browser/device checks, demo and submission materials.

If there are fewer people, combine science with gameplay/QA; combine environment art with UI; keep integration ownership with the technical lead. Agree on data formats and interfaces early. Work in separate files/folders, integrate frequently, and avoid multiple people editing the same scene or core script at once.

AI workflow proposed in the source chat:
- Claude Code: primary implementation and architecture.
- Codex: independent review, debugging, and implementation support.
- Gemini: research, calculations, and scenario design.
- Antigravity: visual direction, interface concepts, and asset workflow.
Use one shared repository and clearly assign file ownership; do not let every tool make overlapping edits to the same files.

14. KEY RISKS AND MITIGATIONS

- Scope grows beyond available time: finish one mission and one map before adding content; cut P2 first.
- Systems become hard to integrate: define shared data structures and sol-update order at the start; keep one integration owner.
- Art delays gameplay: graybox early and replace placeholders incrementally; prioritize readable silhouettes.
- Browser build is left until the end: make a blank web build during setup and keep checking the target build.
- Player cannot tell why a resource changed: show per-sol breakdowns, forecasts, and event history.
- Science feels inaccurate or overly complex: distinguish verified facts from game abstractions; use concise explanations and citations in project documentation.
- Decisions feel like traps: show likely trade-offs, provide warnings, and avoid hidden instant failure.
